12 "Healthy" Foods That Are Actually Ultra-Processed

"Natural." "High protein." "No added sugar." None of these claims tell you how processed a food is — and that is exactly the point.

Plenty of healthy foods are actually ultra-processed. The health claims on the front of a package — natural, high protein, low fat, wholegrain, plant-based — are marketing language. They describe one nutrient, or one ingredient, or nothing measurable at all. None of them describes how the food was made.

The NOVA classification system asks a different question: was this made in a kitchen, or in a factory using ingredients and processes you would never have at home? A food lands in NOVA Group 4 — ultra-processed — when it contains substances you would not cook with: protein isolates, modified starches, emulsifiers, artificial sweeteners, flavourings, colourings.1

That is why a chocolate bar and a "high protein" bar can end up in the same group. Here are 12 products that most people put in the healthy column, what the label promises, and what the ingredient list actually says.

Why "Healthy" Labels Don't Mean Minimally Processed

Front-of-package claims and processing level measure completely different things, and no regulation requires them to agree.

"High protein" is a nutrient threshold. A bar hits it whether the protein comes from nuts or from whey protein isolate refined in a factory. "Low fat" is a nutrient ceiling — and removing fat from yogurt usually means adding sweeteners, thickeners and flavourings to replace the texture and taste that fat provided. "Natural" has no fixed legal meaning in most markets. "Wholegrain" describes one ingredient in a list that may run to twenty.

None of these claims is a lie. They are simply answering a question you were not asking. NOVA asks the question you were: how far is this from food?

Key takeaway: A health claim describes one property of a food. The ingredient list describes what the food actually is. When the two disagree, the ingredient list is the one that tells you about processing.

The 12 Hidden Ultra-Processed Foods

1. Protein bars

The claim: High protein. Natural energy. Fuel for your workout.

The reality: Most protein bars are built from whey, soy or pea protein isolate — protein stripped out of its original food by industrial processing. Add sugar alcohols such as maltitol, glycerol as a humectant, emulsifiers to bind it and flavourings to make it taste like cookie dough, and you have a textbook NOVA Group 4 product. Protein isolates are one of the clearest single markers of ultra-processing in the NOVA framework.

Closer to whole food: A handful of nuts, or a bar whose ingredient list is dates, nuts and cocoa.

2. Flavoured oat and nut milks

The claim: Plant-based. Dairy-free. Barista quality.

The reality: The oats are the least interesting part. Commercial oat milk commonly contains rapeseed or sunflower oil for mouthfeel, dipotassium phosphate as an acidity regulator, gellan gum as a stabiliser, added calcium carbonate, and flavourings — none of which exist in a home kitchen. Vanilla and chocolate versions add sugars and more flavourings on top.

Closer to whole food: Unsweetened versions whose list reads oats, water, salt. They exist, and they are usually shelved next to the flavoured ones.

3. Vegetable and lentil chips

The claim: Made with real vegetables. Baked, not fried.

The reality: In most vegetable crisps the vegetable arrives as a powder, and the body of the chip is potato or corn starch. The ingredient list typically runs starch, vegetable oil, vegetable powder, sugar, salt, flavouring, colouring. A serving delivers roughly the same profile as a potato crisp with a green tint and a better story.

Closer to whole food: Roasted chickpeas, or actual vegetables sliced and roasted at home.

4. Low-fat and "diet" yogurts

The claim: 0% fat. Light. Only 60 calories.

The reality: Fat carries flavour and texture, so removing it creates two problems manufacturers solve with additives. Expect aspartame or sucralose for sweetness, modified maize starch or gelatin for body, and flavourings plus colourings to suggest fruit. Plain yogurt — just milk and live cultures — sits in NOVA Group 1. The 0% fruit version usually sits in Group 4.

Closer to whole food: Plain yogurt with fruit you cut up yourself.

5. Bottled smoothies

The claim: Two of your five a day. Nothing but fruit.

The reality: This one genuinely varies, which is why it is worth checking rather than assuming. Some bottled smoothies really are blended fruit and nothing else, which keeps them out of Group 4. Many others are built from fruit purée and juice from concentrate, with added juice concentrates used as a sweetener and stabilisers to stop separation. The word "concentrate" appearing several times is the signal to look closer.

Closer to whole food: Whole fruit. Blending is fine; the concentrates are the issue.

6. Wholegrain sliced bread

The claim: Wholegrain. High in fibre. Source of B vitamins.

The reality: The flour may well be wholegrain. The rest of the list is where it goes wrong: emulsifiers such as E471 or DATEM, preservatives such as calcium propionate, added gluten, dough conditioners and often added sugar. Wholegrain is a description of one ingredient, not of the loaf. We looked at this in detail in our guide to whether bread is ultra-processed.

Closer to whole food: Real sourdough — flour, water, salt, starter.

7. Plant-based meat substitutes

The claim: Meat-free. Better for you and the planet.

The reality: Making pea protein behave like mince requires soy or pea protein isolate, methylcellulose as a binder, flavourings, colourings such as beetroot extract, and often coconut oil for fat mimicry. By NOVA's definition this is squarely Group 4 — it is a formulation assembled from extracted substances.

Worth being fair about: NOVA measures processing, not virtue. A plant-based burger may still beat a processed meat product on saturated fat, and the environmental case is a separate question entirely. It is ultra-processed and it may be the better choice in a given meal. Both can be true.

Closer to whole food: Beans, lentils, tofu and tempeh, which are minimally processed and have been feeding people for centuries.

8. Coated rice cakes

The claim: Low calorie. Light snack.

The reality: A plain rice cake is close to just puffed rice. Add a chocolate or yogurt-flavoured coating and you add sugar, palm oil, emulsifiers, flavourings and often colourings. The cake is fine. The coating is what moves the product into Group 4.

Closer to whole food: The uncoated version, with something you put on it yourself.

9. "Healthy" breakfast cereals

The claim: Wholegrain. High fibre. Fortified with 8 vitamins and iron.

The reality: Bran flakes, wheat biscuits and most granolas are made by extrusion — grain forced through industrial dies under heat and pressure — then sweetened, flavoured and sprayed with vitamins. Heavy fortification is itself a quiet signal: nutrients are often being added back because processing removed them. We scanned several of these in our breakfast foods roundup.

Closer to whole food: Plain rolled oats, which are one ingredient.

10. Bottled salad dressings

The claim: Light vinaigrette. Made with olive oil.

The reality: It may well contain olive oil, somewhere down the list after water and rapeseed oil. Around them sit modified starch, xanthan gum, emulsifiers, preservatives, and in "light" versions, sweeteners to replace the fat. A dressing you make at home is oil, vinegar, mustard and salt — all NOVA Group 2 culinary ingredients.

Closer to whole food: Oil and vinegar, shaken in a jar. It takes about twenty seconds.

11. Fruit snacks, strips and "fruit" bars

The claim: Made with real fruit. One of your five a day. Perfect for lunchboxes.

The reality: The fruit is usually purée from concentrate, joined by glucose syrup, gelling agents such as pectin used industrially, acidity regulators and concentrated fruit or vegetable juice for colour. The result is closer in structure to a sweet than to fruit, which is what makes this category so effective at reaching children's lunchboxes.

Closer to whole food: An actual piece of fruit, or plain dried fruit with no added syrup.

12. "No added sugar" squashes and soft drinks

The claim: No added sugar. Sugar-free. Zero.

The reality: The claim is accurate and slightly beside the point. Removing sugar means adding sucralose, acesulfame K or aspartame, alongside preservatives, acidity regulators and colourings. Artificial sweeteners are among the most reliable single indicators of NOVA Group 4 in the entire framework — their presence means the product is a formulation, not a food.

Closer to whole food: Water, or water with fruit in it.

How to Spot a Hidden Ultra-Processed Food in 10 Seconds

You do not need to memorise additive codes. Three quick checks catch most Group 4 products:

1. Turn the package over first. The front is advertising. The back is regulated. Read the ingredient list before you read any claim.

2. Look for the five families. Scan for words ending in -ose you do not recognise, anything called an isolate or concentrate, gums and modified starches, emulsifiers, and sweeteners. Any of these makes Group 4 likely.

3. Apply the kitchen test. Ask whether you could buy each ingredient and use it at home. Flour, yes. Olive oil, yes. Methylcellulose, dipotassium phosphate, soy protein isolate — no. If the answer is no more than once or twice, you are holding an ultra-processed food. Our full guide to identifying ultra-processed foods goes deeper on this.

Does NOVA Group 4 Always Mean Unhealthy?

No — and it is worth being precise about this, because overstating the case is how good advice gets ignored.

NOVA classifies foods by how they are made, not by how nutritious they are. A fortified wholegrain cereal is ultra-processed and still delivers fibre and iron. A high-protein yogurt is ultra-processed and still delivers protein. Neither is poison, and no single product is going to decide anyone's health.

What the research points to is the pattern rather than the item. In a tightly controlled inpatient trial, people eating an ultra-processed diet consumed around 500 more calories a day than the same people eating a minimally processed diet matched for nutrients — and gained weight — without reporting feeling any fuller.2 Larger population studies have linked higher ultra-processed intake to increased risk of overweight and obesity.3 We covered the mechanism in our piece on ultra-processed foods and weight gain.

So the useful goal is not zero. It is knowing which foods in your basket are ultra-processed, so the proportion is a choice you are making rather than one the packaging made for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are protein bars ultra-processed?

Most protein bars are ultra-processed, classified as NOVA Group 4. The usual reason is protein isolate — protein extracted from milk, soy or peas by industrial processing — combined with sugar alcohols, emulsifiers and flavourings. Bars made only from dates, nuts and cocoa are not ultra-processed.

Is oat milk ultra-processed?

It depends on the ingredient list. Oat milk containing only oats, water and salt is not ultra-processed. Most commercial versions add oils, stabilisers such as gellan gum, acidity regulators and flavourings, which places them in NOVA Group 4.

Does "natural" mean a food isn't ultra-processed?

No. "Natural" has no consistent legal definition in most markets and makes no claim about processing. A product labelled natural can contain protein isolates, flavourings and emulsifiers and still be NOVA Group 4.

Why are vitamins added to ultra-processed foods?

Fortification often replaces nutrients lost during processing. Heavy fortification is therefore a useful signal: when a label advertises many added vitamins and minerals, it frequently indicates the base product was refined enough to need them added back.

The Bottom Line

The most heavily processed products in a supermarket are often not in the snack aisle. They are in the health food section, wearing words like natural, high protein and no added sugar — because those are the products that need a claim to sell.

None of this requires you to become an ingredients expert. It requires one habit: turn the package over and read the back before you believe the front. When the list is long and full of things you could not buy separately, you are holding an ultra-processed food, whatever the front says.
