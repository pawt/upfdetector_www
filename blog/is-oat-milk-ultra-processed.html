Is Oat Milk Ultra-Processed? It Depends on Three Ingredients

Some cartons list three ingredients. Others list twelve. Same shelf, same name, different NOVA group — and the carton won't tell you which one you're holding.

Oats and water are the whole recipe — until a manufacturer needs it to foam, keep for months and not split in hot coffee.

Oat milk has become the default plant milk in coffee shops and supermarket fridges, and it arrived with a wholesome reputation: oats are a whole grain, the packaging is beige, and the marketing talks about the planet. So is oat milk ultra-processed, or is it just oats and water?

The honest answer is it depends on the ingredient list — more than for almost any other food. Oat milk made from oats, water and salt is not ultra-processed. Most supermarket oat milk, and virtually every "barista" version, adds vegetable oil, an acidity regulator called dipotassium phosphate, and a stabiliser such as gellan gum. Those three ingredients move the carton into NOVA Group 4 — ultra-processed.

Two cartons on the same shelf, with the same word on the front, can sit in different NOVA groups. This article shows you exactly what separates them, why those ingredients are in there, and how to spot the difference in the ten seconds you spend in the chilled aisle.

How Oat Milk Is Made (and Why It Tastes Sweet)

Understanding the answer starts with understanding that oat milk is not oats soaked in water. It is an engineered product, even in its simplest form.

Commercial oat milk is made by milling oats into water, then adding enzymes — typically alpha-amylase, sometimes beta-amylase or amyloglucosidase — that break the oat starch down into smaller sugars, mostly maltose.1 This step is what makes oat milk drinkable rather than a thick porridge, and it is why oat milk tastes noticeably sweet without any added sugar. The mixture is then filtered to remove the insoluble fibre and pulp, and what's left is the "oat base" — usually around 10% oats by weight, the rest water.2

That's the base. What a manufacturer adds to the base is what decides the NOVA classification.

Worth knowing: the enzyme step means oat milk is not nutritionally equivalent to a bowl of oats. Most of the fibre leaves with the pulp, and part of the starch is already converted to sugar before it reaches you. It can still be minimally processed under NOVA — but it isn't a whole food in a carton.

What the NOVA Classification Says About Oat Milk

The NOVA classification groups foods by the extent and purpose of their processing, not by their nutrient content.3 The practical test its authors give is simple: a product is ultra-processed if its ingredient list contains either food substances you'd never find in a kitchen (protein isolates, hydrolysed proteins, maltodextrin, hydrogenated oils) or "cosmetic" additives whose job is to make the product look, taste or feel better — flavourings, colours, emulsifiers, emulsifying salts, sweeteners, thickeners, gelling agents, foaming agents and the like.3

Applied to oat milk, the line falls here:

  • Not ultra-processed: oats, water, salt — and optionally added calcium and vitamins. Fortification with vitamins and minerals is not on NOVA's list of ultra-processing markers. Depending on who is classifying, this sits in NOVA Group 1 (minimally processed) or Group 3 (processed, because salt has been added). Either way, it is not Group 4.
  • Borderline: oats, water, salt and a vegetable oil. Oil is a culinary ingredient (NOVA Group 2), so adding it alone makes a Group 3 product. In practice, though, oil in oat milk is almost never alone — see the next point.
  • NOVA Group 4 — ultra-processed: any oat milk with dipotassium phosphate, gellan gum or another gum, "flavourings" or "natural flavour", added sugar alongside other additives, or lecithin as an emulsifier. That is the majority of what's on the shelf, and essentially every barista edition.

Key rule: if the ingredient list of an oat milk goes beyond oats, water, salt, and added vitamins or calcium, look for three names — a vegetable oil, dipotassium phosphate, and gellan gum. Any one of the last two makes it NOVA Group 4. Find all three and you have a typical supermarket oat milk.

Notice what is not the problem. Calcium carbonate and the vitamins are fortification — they're added because oat milk has almost none of the calcium or B12 that dairy milk has, and NOVA does not count them as markers of ultra-processing. The classification hinges on the oil, the phosphate and, where present, the gum.

The Oat Milk Ingredients That Signal Ultra-Processing

Each additive in oat milk is there to solve a specific engineering problem. Knowing the problem tells you why the ingredient is unlikely to disappear from the recipe.

  • Rapeseed, sunflower or canola oil — oats have very little fat, so plain oat milk is thin. Oil gives it the mouthfeel of whole dairy milk and lets it foam. A culinary ingredient on its own, but it needs the next two to stay mixed in.
  • Dipotassium phosphate (E340) — an acidity regulator and emulsifying salt. Coffee is acidic and hot, and oat protein curdles in it; dipotassium phosphate buffers the pH so the milk doesn't split in your latte. It is the single most reliable marker of a NOVA 4 oat milk.
  • Gellan gum (E418) — a gelling and stabilising agent that keeps the oil and oat solids suspended so the carton doesn't separate over a long shelf life. Sometimes appears alongside or instead of guar gum, xanthan gum or locust bean gum.
  • Sunflower or soy lecithin — an emulsifier that helps the oil stay dispersed. Less common in oat milk than in almond milk, but it appears.
  • "Flavourings" or "natural flavour" — a cosmetic additive by NOVA's definition, whatever the word "natural" implies. Standard in vanilla, chocolate and "extra creamy" versions.
  • Sugar, cane sugar, glucose syrup — unnecessary in a drink that's already sweet from maltose, but common in flavoured and some "original" versions. Sugar alone would make a Group 3 product; in oat milk it never travels alone.
  • Maltodextrin — occasionally used as a bulking agent. A hallmark NOVA 4 substance.

Two ingredients that look chemical but are not ultra-processing markers under NOVA: calcium carbonate and calcium phosphates when used for fortification, and the vitamins (D2, riboflavin, B12) added to mimic dairy's nutritional profile. Don't let a long-sounding list of fortificants distract you from the three ingredients that actually decide the classification.

Is Barista Oat Milk Ultra-Processed?

Yes — almost without exception. "Barista" is not a flavour; it's an engineering specification. A barista oat milk has to steam to a stable microfoam, hold that foam long enough to pour latte art, and not curdle when it hits a hot, acidic espresso. Plain oats and water can't do any of that.

So a barista formulation adds more oil for foam structure, dipotassium phosphate to stop the curdling, and usually a gum to keep the emulsion stable. The result is a drink that behaves beautifully in coffee and classifies as NOVA Group 4. If the oat milk you drink is mainly the one your coffee shop uses, assume it is ultra-processed unless you've read the carton.

A handful of brands now sell "barista" versions without oil or phosphates. They typically foam less well, and their ingredient lists are the proof: if it foams like dairy and lists three ingredients, read the list again.

Doesn't the Enzyme Process Make All Oat Milk Ultra-Processed?

This is the strongest objection, and it deserves a straight answer. Enzymatic hydrolysis — using amylase to convert starch into maltose — is an industrial step that you cannot replicate in a home kitchen. Some critics argue that makes every commercial oat milk ultra-processed regardless of the label.

NOVA, as its authors define it, does not work that way. It classifies by the substances in the final product, and it treats added enzymes as processing aids, not as ingredients — the same way it treats the rennet in cheese or the yeast in bread.3 Amylase breaking down oat starch is also chemically the same reaction the amylase in your saliva starts when you chew oats. Under the framework, a three-ingredient oat milk is processed but not ultra-processed.

What the objection gets right is that oat milk is not a whole food and shouldn't be treated as one. The reasonable position is this: a clean-label oat milk is a minimally processed beverage, a barista oat milk is an ultra-processed one, and neither is a bowl of porridge. If you drink oat milk because you like it or need a dairy alternative, choosing the short list is a meaningful improvement. If you drink it for the oats, eat the oats.

Does It Matter If Oat Milk Is Ultra-Processed?

For a food you might pour into two or three coffees a day, every day, it's worth asking. A few things are known, and a few are not.

Emulsifiers and stabilisers are the most-studied concern. In mice, common dietary emulsifiers altered the gut microbiota and promoted low-grade inflammation and metabolic changes.4 A controlled feeding study in healthy adults found that two weeks of one emulsifier, carboxymethylcellulose, measurably changed the gut microbiome and its metabolites.5 Gellan gum specifically has been studied far less, so the honest position is "plausibly similar, not proven".

Phosphate additives are a separate issue. Inorganic phosphate from additives such as dipotassium phosphate is absorbed far more completely than phosphate naturally present in food, and high intakes are a recognised concern for people with reduced kidney function; there is also evidence linking them to cardiovascular risk in the general population.6 The amount in a splash of oat milk is small — the point is that it adds to a phosphate-additive load that already comes from processed meat, cola and packaged bakery goods.

Ultra-processed diets as a whole drive overeating. In the NIH's controlled trial, people eating an ultra-processed diet consumed about 500 more calories a day than the same people on an unprocessed diet matched for nutrients.7 Oat milk is a minor contributor to that pattern on its own — but the pattern is built from minor contributors.

None of this makes barista oat milk dangerous. It makes it an ultra-processed food, with the same reasons to limit it as any other — and it means the clean-label version is not just marketing, but a genuinely different product.

How to Choose a Non-Ultra-Processed Oat Milk

The three-line test

Turn the carton around. If the ingredient list is three items — oats, water, salt — or those three plus calcium and vitamins, it's not ultra-processed. If you see dipotassium phosphate, gellan gum, or flavourings, it's NOVA Group 4. That's the whole test, and it takes ten seconds.

What the front of the carton is telling you

  • "Barista" — almost always oil plus phosphate. Treat as ultra-processed by default.
  • "Original" or "Whole" — usually oil plus fortification, often a gum. Read the list.
  • "Unsweetened" — says nothing about processing. Unsweetened oat milk can still contain every additive above.
  • "No oils", "no gums", "clean label", "3 ingredients" — the claims that actually correlate with a short list. Still verify.
  • "Organic" — an organic gellan gum is still gellan gum. Organic certification restricts some additives but doesn't guarantee a clean list.

Make it at home

Homemade oat milk is one of the easiest things to make: blend one part rolled oats with four parts cold water for about thirty seconds, strain through a fine cloth, add a pinch of salt. It costs pennies and contains nothing you didn't put in. Two honest caveats: without the enzyme step it's less sweet and can turn slimy if over-blended or heated, so it suits cereal and cold drinks better than a latte. If your main use is coffee, a clean-label commercial carton is the more practical choice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Oatly ultra-processed?

Oatly's Barista Edition is ultra-processed under NOVA: its ingredient list includes rapeseed oil and dipotassium phosphate alongside oat base, fortificants and salt. Oatly's other products vary by market and recipe, so check the specific carton — the presence of dipotassium phosphate or a gum is the deciding factor.

Is oat milk high in sugar?

Unsweetened oat milk typically contains around 3–4 g of sugar per 100 ml, almost all of it maltose produced when enzymes break down the oat starch during manufacturing. It is not "added sugar" on the label, but it is sugar the oats didn't have before processing. Flavoured versions add more on top.

Is oat milk worse than cow's milk?

They are different products. Plain cow's milk is NOVA Group 1 and has roughly three times the protein of oat milk. Clean-label oat milk is also minimally processed. Barista and flavoured oat milks are NOVA Group 4. If you choose oat milk for dietary, ethical or environmental reasons, the ingredient list decides how processed your choice is.

Is homemade oat milk ultra-processed?

No. Oats blended with water and strained is a NOVA Group 1 food. It won't foam or hold up in hot coffee the way commercial oat milk does, because it lacks the oil and phosphate that make that possible.

Which oat milk is not ultra-processed?

Any oat milk whose full ingredient list is oats, water and salt, with or without added calcium and vitamins. In the UK, Plenish sells one with exactly that list; other markets have equivalents, usually labelled "no oils" or "no gums". Check the list rather than the brand, because recipes change.

The Bottom Line

Is oat milk ultra-processed? Some of it is, and the difference is entirely on the back of the carton. Oats, water and salt is not ultra-processed. Add a vegetable oil, dipotassium phosphate and gellan gum — the three ingredients that make a carton foam, keep and survive hot coffee — and it is NOVA Group 4.

Most of what is sold as oat milk, and nearly all of what coffee shops use, is the second kind. The first kind exists, sits on the same shelf, and takes ten seconds to identify. You don't have to give up oat milk. You just need to know which one you're drinking.
