Is Peanut Butter Ultra-Processed? Read the Label

A jar of ground peanuts and a sweetened, stabilised spread can look almost identical. Seven real products show what to check on the back.

The quick answer

Peanut butter is not automatically ultra-processed. A jar made only from peanuts is minimally processed. Adding salt, sugar or ordinary vegetable oil does not by itself make it ultra-processed. Ingredients such as hydrogenated oils, emulsifiers or maltodextrin are the markers to look for.

The useful question is which recipe you are buying. “Smooth”, “crunchy” and the brand name won't answer it. Below, we compare specific UK, US and Australian products using their manufacturers' published ingredients.

When does peanut butter become ultra-processed?

NOVA groups foods by processing. Roasting and grinding are compatible with minimal processing; putting the result in a supermarket jar doesn't change that. Applying the NOVA authors' ingredient guidance gives these three patterns:

Group 1 · Minimally processed

Peanuts only. Smooth or crunchy makes no difference.

Group 3 · Processed

Peanuts with culinary ingredients such as salt, sugar or ordinary oil, without ultra-processing markers.

Group 4 · Ultra-processed

A formulation containing markers such as hydrogenated oils, maltodextrin, flavourings or emulsifiers.

Ingredient count is not the test. A five-ingredient recipe can fit Group 3. A shorter one can contain an ultra-processing marker.

UK: Meridian and Whole Earth

Meridian Smooth Peanut Butter 280g

Ingredients: Peanuts (100%).

Our assessment: Group 1. Meridian describes roasting peanuts in their skins and grinding them. This specific jar contains no added salt, sugar or oil. It is a straightforward example of minimal processing.

Whole Earth Smooth Peanut Butter 454g

Ingredients: Roasted peanuts (96%), sustainable palm oil, sea salt.

Our assessment: Group 3, based on the listed ingredients. The added oil and salt make this processed. The list does not name a hydrogenated oil or emulsifier. Palm oil alone is not a reason to call it Group 4.

These two jars show why “contains added oil” and “ultra-processed” are different judgments. If you prefer peanuts alone, Meridian fits that preference; Whole Earth's listed recipe still lacks an explicit ultra-processing marker.

US: Smucker's and two different Jif recipes

Smucker's Natural Creamy Peanut Butter

Ingredients: Peanuts; contains 1% or less of salt.

Our assessment: Group 3. Salt is the only addition to peanuts. That moves this jar out of the peanuts-only category, but it does not make it ultra-processed.

Jif Creamy Peanut Butter

Ingredients: Roasted peanuts, sugar; 2% or less of: molasses, fully hydrogenated vegetable oils (rapeseed and soybean), mono and diglycerides, salt.

Our assessment: Group 4. The fully hydrogenated oils and the emulsifiers mono and diglycerides are the relevant markers. The classification does not hinge on sugar or molasses.

Jif Natural Creamy Peanut Butter Spread

Ingredients: Peanuts, sugar, palm oil; 2% or less of: salt, molasses.

Our assessment: Group 3, based on the listed ingredients. This is a different formulation from regular Jif Creamy. Its published list contains culinary ingredients without the hydrogenated oils or mono and diglycerides above.

Calling all Jif peanut butter ultra-processed would miss that difference. Equally, Jif Natural is not just peanuts: the manufacturer describes a spread containing 90% peanuts. Read the full variant name, then the ingredients.

Australia: Mayver's and Bega Light

Mayver's 100% Australian Smooth Peanut Butter 375g

Ingredients: Roasted hi-oleic peanuts (99.7%) and sea salt.

Our assessment: Group 3. This is peanuts with salt. “100% Australian” describes the product's origin; it does not mean the jar contains 100% peanuts. The manufacturer gives the peanut content as 99.7%.

Bega Light Peanut Spread — Smooth

Ingredients: Roasted peanuts (62%), maltodextrin, sugar, vegetable oil (contains antioxidant 307b), salt, emulsifier 471.

Our assessment: Group 4. Maltodextrin and the explicitly listed emulsifier are the markers here. Bega advertises this Light spread as having 25% less fat than its regular peanut butter; a reduced-fat claim does not describe processing.

The Bega example is specifically Light Peanut Spread, Smooth. Its ingredients should not be attributed to Bega Original, Whipped or No Added Sugar or Salt products, which have separate recipes.

Does palm oil or “no stir” mean ultra-processed?

No. The distinction between Whole Earth Smooth and regular Jif Creamy is useful: one lists palm oil, while the other explicitly lists fully hydrogenated oils and emulsifiers. Those are not interchangeable ingredient descriptions.

Nor can you classify a jar by whether oil separates. Jif says its Natural spread needs no stirring, yet the published recipe uses palm oil without listing the regular version's emulsifiers. Meridian says its peanuts-only butter can separate and be stirred back together. Appearance is a prompt to read the label, not a NOVA test.

A quick peanut butter shopping checklist

  1. Start with the exact jar. Match the country, variant and current ingredients, rather than relying on a verdict about the brand.
  2. For the simplest option, look for peanuts only. Peanuts with salt is also an option without ultra-processing markers.
  3. Check additions by name. Compare them with the examples above; don't reject every recipe containing sugar or oil.
  4. Read nutrition separately. Compare salt or sodium, sugars and saturated fat on an equivalent serving or per-100g basis. NOVA is not a complete nutrition score.

Frequently asked questions

Is natural peanut butter always minimally processed?

Check the recipe. In our examples, Meridian is peanuts only, Smucker's adds salt, and Jif Natural adds sugar, palm oil, salt and molasses. The word “natural” does not tell you which of those you are getting.

Is crunchy peanut butter less processed than smooth?

Texture alone doesn't determine the group. The question is what was added and how it was made, not whether there are peanut pieces left in the jar.

Does a Group 4 rating mean I must stop eating it?

The rating describes processing, not a personal instruction to avoid a food. Use it alongside nutrition information, your dietary needs and the rest of your meals. If your aim is fewer ultra-processed products, switching to a simpler peanut butter is one practical option.
