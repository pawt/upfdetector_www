Is Greek Yogurt Ultra-Processed? Check the Label

A plain tub made from milk and cultures and a sweetened high-protein pot can share the same aisle—and look equally wholesome. Six real products show what separates them.

The quick answer

Plain Greek yogurt is usually not ultra-processed. Milk, cream and live cultures fit NOVA Group 1, whether the yogurt is full-fat or 0% fat. A flavored or “high-protein” yogurt may be ultra-processed when its recipe includes markers such as flavorings, modified starch or non-sugar sweeteners. The claim on the front does not decide it; the ingredients on the back do.

“Greek yogurt” and the British spelling “Greek yoghurt” describe a thick cultured dairy product, but they do not guarantee one recipe. Even two products from the same brand can land in different NOVA groups.

What makes Greek yogurt ultra-processed?

NOVA classifies food by the nature and purpose of processing, not by calories, protein or fat. Pasteurising milk, fermenting it with cultures and straining off some whey are traditional processes. They do not make yogurt ultra-processed.

Group 1 · Minimally processed

Milk or skim milk, sometimes cream, plus live yogurt cultures. Straining for thickness is compatible with Group 1.

Group 3 · Processed

Plain yogurt combined with culinary ingredients such as sugar or fruit, without ingredients characteristic of ultra-processed formulations.

Group 4 · Ultra-processed

A formulation containing markers such as flavorings, modified starches, non-sugar sweeteners, colors or protein isolates.

Fat level is not the test. Manufacturers can make thick 0% Greek yogurt simply by straining cultured skim milk. Conversely, “no added sugar” and “15g protein” do not prevent a flavored product from being Group 4.

UK: FAGE plain and strawberry

FAGE Total 5%

Ingredients: Pasteurised skimmed milk, cream, live active yoghurt cultures.

Our assessment: Group 1. This is cultured dairy that has been strained for thickness. Cream adjusts the fat content but is still a familiar dairy ingredient; there are no sweeteners, thickeners or flavorings.

FAGE Fruits Strawberry

Ingredients: Strained yoghurt (pasteurised skimmed milk, cream, cultures), strawberry puree (8%), sugar, water, maize starch, black carrot juice concentrate, natural flavourings.

Our assessment: Group 4. The named natural flavorings are the clearest ultra-processing marker. Strawberry and sugar alone would not establish Group 4; the formulation as a whole is what matters.

These products share a brand and a strained-yogurt base, yet their ingredient lists tell different stories. “Natural ingredients” on the front of a flavored pot is not a NOVA category.

US: Oikos Plain and Triple Zero Vanilla

Oikos Blended Greek Nonfat Yogurt, Plain

Ingredients: Cultured nonfat milk; yogurt cultures.

Our assessment: Group 1. This is a useful counterexample to the idea that all fat-free yogurt needs additives. The published recipe is simply cultured nonfat milk.

Oikos Triple Zero Vanilla

Ingredients: Cultured Grade A nonfat milk, water, natural flavors; less than 1% of tapioca starch, stevia leaf extract, lemon juice concentrate, sea salt and vitamin D3; yogurt cultures.

Our assessment: Group 4. Natural flavors and a non-sugar sweetener are markers of an ultra-processed formulation. “Zero added sugar,” “zero artificial sweeteners” and “0% fat” are separate claims; none answers the NOVA question.

Both are nonfat and high in protein. One gets there with cultured milk alone; the vanilla pot is engineered for sweetness and flavor without added sugar. Protein grams do not reveal that distinction.

Australia: Chobani Natural and Farmers Union Strawberry

Chobani Natural Greek Yogurt

Ingredients: Skim milk, cream, live yogurt cultures.

Our assessment: Group 1. Like the plain FAGE example, the thickness and protein come from culturing and straining dairy rather than from a list of formulation ingredients.

Farmers Union Greek Style High Protein Strawberry Yogurt

Ingredients: Milk solids, skim milk, water, cream, strawberry puree (3.7%), thickener 1442, natural flavour, fruit concentrates for color, salt, steviol glycosides and live yogurt cultures.

Our assessment: Group 4. Thickener 1442 is a modified starch; the natural flavor and steviol glycosides add further Group 4 markers. Its high protein and no-added-sugar claims describe nutrients, not processing.

“Greek” versus “Greek style” can describe different production traditions, but the wording alone does not determine the NOVA group. The exact recipe does.

Plain, flavored and fruit-added are not the same thing

A spoonful of berries added to plain Greek yogurt at home does not turn it into an ultra-processed food. Nor does sugar automatically do so. Under NOVA, fruit and sugar are ordinary food or culinary ingredients; flavorings, modified starches, non-sugar sweeteners and other cosmetic additives are the stronger clues.

This is why “flavored yogurt is always ultra-processed” is too blunt. Many supermarket versions are Group 4, including the three flavored examples above, but you still classify the recipe—not the flavor name.

Does high-protein Greek yogurt count as ultra-processed?

Not automatically. Traditional straining removes some liquid whey, leaving more protein in the same weight of yogurt. A plain Greek yogurt can therefore be naturally high in protein with only milk and cultures.

Check whether the manufacturer instead adds milk protein concentrate, whey protein or an isolate, then look at the rest of the recipe. NOVA specifically treats protein isolates and many cosmetic additives as signs of ultra-processing. The phrase “high protein” alone tells you nothing about which route was used.

A quick Greek yogurt shopping checklist

  1. Start with the exact pot. Recipes vary by country, flavor, fat level and package format—even within one brand.
  2. For the simplest option, look for milk and cultures. Cream is also compatible with a minimally processed plain yogurt.
  3. Do not reject 0% automatically. Plain nonfat Greek yogurt can be made without sweeteners or thickeners.
  4. Look past “natural” and “no added sugar.” Check for flavorings, modified starch, sweeteners, colors and protein isolates.
  5. Read nutrition separately. Compare protein, sugars, saturated fat and serving size according to your needs. NOVA is a processing classification, not a complete nutrition score.

Frequently asked questions

Is plain Greek yogurt a processed food?

It is processed in the everyday sense: milk is pasteurised, fermented and strained. Under NOVA, plain yogurt made from milk and cultures remains Group 1, minimally processed food. “Processed” and “ultra-processed” are not synonyms.

Is 0% Greek yogurt ultra-processed?

Not necessarily. Oikos Plain in the US lists cultured nonfat milk, while Chobani Natural Light in Australia lists skim milk and cultures. A 0% flavored product may contain Group 4 markers, so read its ingredients rather than judging the fat claim.

Is Greek-style yogurt more processed than Greek yogurt?

The naming rules and production methods vary between markets. For NOVA, what matters is how the specific product is made and what is added—not whether the front says “Greek” or “Greek style.”

Is flavored Greek yogurt always ultra-processed?

No. Yogurt with fruit and sugar can fit Group 3 if it contains no ultra-processing markers. Many commercial flavored products do contain flavorings, modified starches or sweeteners and therefore fit Group 4. Check the exact recipe.
